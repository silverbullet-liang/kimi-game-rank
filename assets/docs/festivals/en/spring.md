# Spring Festival

## Overview

The first day of the first lunar month, the most important traditional festival in China and one kept across East Asia and in Chinese communities worldwide. The skin covers nine consecutive days from the first: the fifth is *powu*, the day the ban on sweeping and cooking is lifted; the seventh is the birthday of humankind; the ninth, in Fujian and Taiwan, is the Jade Emperor's birthday. On 4 December 2024 "Spring Festival, social practices of the Chinese people in celebration of the traditional new year" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity; in December 2023 the 78th UN General Assembly had made the lunar new year a UN holiday.

## Origins

The festival grows out of ancient rites at the turn of the year, offerings and prayers for the coming harvest. The *Taichu Calendar* issued under Emperor Wu of Han fixed the first month as the start of the year, and the arrangement has held for more than two thousand years. When the Republic adopted the Gregorian calendar in the early 1910s, 1 January took the name New Year's Day and the lunar first day was called the Spring Festival. It is not one day but a stretch: shopping and preparation in the twelfth month, the vigil on New Year's Eve, and the Lantern Festival on the fifteenth of the first month, roughly a month in all — and that whole span is what the UNESCO nomination described.

## Customs

1. **Couplets, blessings and New Year prints**: red paper couplets on the door frames, door gods and the character for blessing on the panels, fresh prints indoors — the house turned red on every face.
2. **The reunion dinner and festival food**: dumplings in the north; rice cakes, sweet rice balls and eight-treasure rice in the south; fish kept whole, and something always left over.
3. **New Year calls and red envelopes**: greetings to elders early on the first morning, red envelopes of money to children, then days of visiting relatives and friends.
4. **Temple fairs, dragons and lions**: fairs in parks and at city god temples with dragon and lion dances, stilt walkers and lantern riddles — the loudest places of the season.
5. **The fifth and the seventh**: on the fifth, firecrackers, sending away poverty and welcoming the god of wealth; on the seventh, a soup of seven vegetables; on the ninth, offerings to the Jade Emperor in Fujian and Taiwan.

## Story

One source of the red paper on the door is the peachwood charm. The *Jingchu Suishiji* records that on the first day of the first month a painted rooster was pasted on the door, reed ropes hung, and peachwood boards set beside them to keep off ghosts. In the tradition behind the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, a great peach tree on Mount Dushuo was guarded by two gods, Shenshu and Yulei, who controlled the ghosts, and peachwood was therefore thought to repel harm. Later the boards became red paper and the gods' names became balanced lines of good wishes, and the charm turned into the couplet. It reads as literature now; it began as protection on a door.

## Theme

The skin uses two lanterns swinging slightly, one octagonal window flower, and four flakes of gold drifting down. The lanterns move, the paper cut holds still, and the flakes fall between them. Spring Festival has essentially one colour, red, but red needs gold to shine; this skin draws that gold — the light in the lanterns, the edge of the paper, the flakes half-way down.
