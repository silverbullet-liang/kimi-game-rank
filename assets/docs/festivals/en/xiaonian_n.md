# Little New Year in Northern China

## Overview

The twenty-third day of the twelfth lunar month, also called the Kitchen God Festival or simply Little New Year. Most of northern China keeps this date; most of southern China keeps the twenty-fourth. "Little" does not mean the day is minor: it marks the start of New Year preparations, ahead of the far larger New Year's Eve and Spring Festival.

## Origins

Offerings to the kitchen god belong to a very old set of rites. The *Book of Rites* lists a stove among the seven sacrifices a ruler made, and the *Analects* has the line "rather than flatter the household shrine, better to flatter the stove" — the god was already being served before the Qin dynasty. Why the offering moved from the twenty-fourth to the twenty-third is usually explained this way: the Qing court made its offering on the twenty-third, princes and metropolitan officials followed, and northern families gradually shifted with them, while the south kept the older date. An old saying describes the same spread: officials on the twenty-third, commoners on the twenty-fourth, boat people on the twenty-fifth.

## Customs

1. **The kitchen god's send-off**: sweets of malt sugar, water and fodder set before the stove; the sweets are sticky and sweet so that the god reports favourably on the household.
2. **Sweeping the dust**: ceilings, windows, bedding — a year of dust cleared in one day, an image of out with the old.
3. **Paper-cut window flowers**: red paper cut into birds, figures and the character for blessing, pasted on newly washed window lattices, the brightest red of a northern winter.
4. **Haircut and bath**: "rich or poor, get a haircut before the New Year", as the saying goes; adults and children both.
5. **Festival shopping and steamed food**: sweets, firecrackers, couplets; steamed buns, date cakes and frozen bean buns put by in the yard for the whole month.

## Story

The kitchen god is the best-known figure of the day. It is said he lives by the stove all year, watching what a family eats and how they behave, and keeping a record of their good and bad acts. On the night of the twenty-third he goes up to report to the Jade Emperor. Fearing a bad report, people offer sticky sweet malt sugar to seal his mouth or sweeten it. The old paper image is then taken down and burned, and a new one pasted up on New Year's Eve — sending him off, and welcoming him back. The tale has no single textual source, yet it has been told at kitchen stoves north and south for centuries.

## Theme

The skin draws one octagonal paper-cut window with a piece of malt sugar beside it. The knife lines inside the lattice are red, the sugar is pale gold, and the two sit side by side on a deep red ground. The paper cut is for people to look at; the sugar is for a god to eat. On this day the noise of the household and the offering at the stove are placed together.
