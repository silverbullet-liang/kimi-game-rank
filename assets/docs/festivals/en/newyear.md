# New Year's Day

## Overview

1 January, the first day of the Gregorian year and a public holiday in most countries. In China it is kept separate from the Lunar New Year: 1 January is New Year's Day, while the first day of the first lunar month is the Spring Festival. The two are often merged in translation, so the names are kept apart here. New Year's Day is a statutory public holiday in China, with one day off.

## Origins

In classical Chinese, *yuandan* meant the first day of the first lunar month. *Yuan* is "beginning" and *dan* is "the rising sun", so the word pictures the first morning of a year. In 1912 the Republic of China adopted the Gregorian calendar, and 1 January was called the "New Year". On 27 September 1949 the First Plenary Session of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference decided that the People's Republic would use the Gregorian calendar; 1 January was formally named New Year's Day, and the first day of the lunar first month was renamed the Spring Festival. Since then the word has referred to the solar new year rather than the lunar one.

## Customs

1. **Counting down**: staying up on 31 December to hear bells, watch fireworks or count the last seconds of the year.
2. **A new calendar**: wall calendars, desk calendars and planners are replaced around this date, and many people write the year's plans on the first page.
3. **A family meal**: dumplings in parts of the north, rice cakes or sweet glutinous rice balls in parts of the south, both carrying a wish for a rising, rounded year.
4. **Greetings**: messages and cards to relatives and friends; in the past decade or so phone messages and short videos have replaced most paper cards.
5. **Public events**: long-distance runs, hill climbs, flag ceremonies and concerts are held in many cities, and the holiday is often joined to a short trip.

## Story

No legend is attached to 1 January in China; what surrounds the date is the story of the calendar itself. The Gregorian calendar descends from the Roman one, and January takes its name from Janus, the Roman god of gates, doorways and all beginnings, shown with two faces — one looking back, one looking forward. Romans made offerings to him at the start of the year. That double face later became the Western image of a month that looks both ways at once.

## Theme

The skin draws only a thin line lying along the horizon, a few short tick marks above it, and a faint band of morning light seeping from behind the line. Nothing in the picture is named. That is the point of the day: the old year has just closed, the new one has not happened yet, and only time itself has been measured again.
