# Qingming Festival

## Overview

Qingming falls between 4 and 6 April in the Gregorian calendar; in 2026 it is 5 April. It is at once one of the twenty-four solar terms and a traditional festival, and the two fall on the same day: Qingming is the only solar term that is also a festival. On 20 May 2006 it was placed on the first national list of intangible cultural heritage with State Council approval, and since 2008 it has been a statutory public holiday.

## Origins

As a solar term, Qingming marks the moment the sun reaches 15 degrees of ecliptic longitude, which normally happens between 4 and 6 April, so the exact date and time shift slightly from year to year. The *Yueling Qishier Hou Jijie*, a Song commentary on the monthly ordinances, glosses the name: "at this time all things are clean and bright." As a festival, the Qingming kept today is the fusion of the solar term with the Cold Food Festival and the Double Third. Cold Food already involved extinguishing fires and eating cold food in the Han dynasty; ancestor graveside rites took shape in the Tang. Because the two dates sat a day or two apart, in the twelfth year of the Dali era (777) Emperor Daizong of Tang decreed that "from now on Cold Food is the same as Qingming", confirming the merger by official document, and in the Song the Double Third faded and the three became one.

## Customs

1. **Grave sweeping**: tidying the mound, weeding and adding earth, setting out food and incense, burning paper or leaving flowers, with the family going together — the main act of the day.
2. **Spring outings**: growth is at its freshest, and after the graves families walk out, look at blossoms and fly kites; mourning and pleasure share the same day.
3. **Willow twigs**: cut willow branches set over the door lintel, or woven into wreaths for the head; a saying warns that one who wears no willow at Qingming will go grey before their time.
4. **Green rice balls**: in the Jiangnan region mugwort or grass juice is kneaded into glutinous rice flour and steamed around a bean-paste filling, green outside and fragrant; in parts of Shanxi wheaten swallow-shaped buns are made instead.
5. **Kites and swings**: an old practice of writing one's ailments on a kite, flying it high and cutting the string to let it go, so that the bad luck goes with it.

## Story

The best-known account of the Cold Food fire ban concerns Jie Zitui. It is said that Chong'er, a prince of Jin in the Spring and Autumn period, spent nineteen years in exile, and that Jie Zitui, who went with him, once cut flesh from his own thigh to feed him. When Chong'er returned and took the throne as Duke Wen of Jin, Jie Zitui asked for no reward and withdrew into Mount Mian with his mother. The duke set fire to the mountain to force him out, and Jie Zitui died holding a tree. Regretting it, the duke ordered that no fire be lit on that day and that only cold food be eaten, and so the custom began. The story comes down in later writings and its details can no longer be verified, but the fire ban, the cold food and the grave offerings did indeed come together around Qingming.

## Theme

The skin uses a grey-green ground, a weeping willow at the upper right, and fine slanted rain falling slowly. The willow hangs down, the rain falls at an angle, and neither looks upward, which is what makes the picture quiet. Both things the festival holds are here: the growing part, in leaf already, and the losing part, in rain that does not stop — in the same season.
