# Weiya

## Overview

The sixteenth day of the twelfth lunar month, observed in Fujian, Taiwan and Chinese communities in Southeast Asia with Minnan roots. *Ya* refers to the twice-monthly offerings made by shops on the second and sixteenth of each lunar month; the sixteenth of the twelfth month is the last of the year, hence *weiya*, the "tail ya".

## Origins

The custom grew out of offerings to the earth god. In the old commercial year a shop set out meat, wine and incense for the earth deity on the second and sixteenth of every month, then divided the food among the staff — twenty-four times a year, the first called the "head ya" and the last the "tail ya". As the year closed, the owner feasted the staff for their work and used the occasion to settle wages and next year's staffing, so the meal was both thanks and negotiation. Commercial life changed, the offerings faded, the banquet stayed, and the word now names the year-end dinner of many companies.

## Customs

1. **Offering to the earth god**: a table at the shop front with meat, wine and rice cakes, incense and firecrackers, in thanks for a year's protection.
2. **The weiya banquet**: the employer hosts the whole staff, once in a courtyard and now usually in a restaurant, with raffles, red envelopes and long-service awards.
3. **Popiah and gua bao**: in Fujian and Taiwan, thin pancakes rolled around vegetables and steamed buns folded around pork belly, shaped like a purse and read as holding wealth in.
4. **The chicken's head**: a poached chicken stood on the table, and the direction its head pointed told the staff what the owner had decided; later, to avoid embarrassment, the head was turned down or removed.
5. **Closing the books**: accounts collected, year-end pay issued and plans for the coming year written up; in the old saying, no debt was carried past weiya.

## Story

What people most often recall about weiya is the head of that chicken. It is said that an employer who did not want to dismiss a worker to his face would point the poached chicken's head at him during the banquet, and the man would understand that he need not return. If the owner wanted to keep everyone, the head was turned toward himself or taken away. The signal came to be thought too blunt, and word was given privately instead. Whether the story is literally true can no longer be checked, but it records something real: in an old trading house, a meal was both a reward and a contract.

## Theme

The skin shows a table at the moment a banquet breaks up: a lamp hanging low, one corner of the tablecloth slipping down, a pair of chopsticks set aside. The lamp is still lit, the cloth has come loose, the chopsticks lie flat and are not raised again. That is the instant weiya names — the year's business finished, the guests gone, only the emptiness of a table that has been eaten from.
