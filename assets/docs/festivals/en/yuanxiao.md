# Lantern Festival

## Overview

The fifteenth day of the first lunar month, also called the Shangyuan Festival, the Lantern Festival or the little first month. It is the first full moon of the lunar year and the close of the New Year season: once the lanterns are up, the holiday is over. Daoism grouped the fifteenths of the first, seventh and tenth months as the three primes, and the first of these is Shangyuan, which is where the alternative name comes from.

## Origins

One common account of the lanterns links them to Buddhism: it is said that Emperor Ming of Han, wishing to show respect for the Buddha, ordered lamps lit in the palace and in monasteries on the night of the fifteenth, and the practice spread from there. The story appears in later records and its details cannot be verified. What is documented is that the *Jingchu Suishiji* of the Liang dynasty already describes making bean gruel with oil on top on the fifteenth to make offering at the doors, so the night had fixed customs by the Southern and Northern Dynasties. Lanterns were lavish in Tang Chang'an — Su Weidao's poem on the night of the fifteenth leaves the line "fire trees and silver flowers close together". The Song extended the lantern nights to five and then six, and pasted riddles onto the lamps, which is where lantern riddles began. In the Ming and Qing, lantern markets became fixed places in the city.

## Customs

1. **Lanterns and lantern fairs**: palace lanterns, revolving horse lanterns and dragon lanterns, each made differently; parks and streets hold fairs.
2. **Lantern riddles**: the puzzle written on a slip and hung under the lamp, taken down when solved to claim a prize, with its own conventions of form and category.
3. **Sweet rice balls**: in the north the filling is rolled in dry flour until it forms a ball; in the south a glutinous wrapper is wrapped around the filling and rolled round. Sesame, bean paste and peanut are the usual fillings.
4. **Dragons, lions and street parades**: dragon lanterns, lion teams, stilt walkers, land boats and yangge — the last large street festival of the season.
5. **Walking away illness**: an old northern custom in which women go out together on the nights of the fifteenth and sixteenth, crossing bridges and walking the city wall, to leave illness behind.

## Story

A widely told story involves Dongfang Shuo, a courtier of Emperor Wu of Han. A palace woman named Yuanxiao, longing for her family, was about to throw herself into a well. To help her, Dongfang Shuo set up a fortune-telling stall in the streets and spread the prediction that the city would burn on the fifteenth; then he advised the emperor that the god of fire liked sweet rice balls, and that the whole city should hang red lanterns, set off fireworks and boil rice balls that night, so that the brightness would stand in place of a real fire. The emperor agreed. Chang'an blazed with light, Yuanxiao's parents came into the city to see it, and the family met. The story appears in later miscellanies and popular telling, not in the official histories.

## Theme

The skin draws a bowl of sweet rice balls with three lanterns of different sizes hanging above. The bowl is near, low and still; the lanterns are far, high and lit. A wide empty space is left between one white bowl and three warm lights. The two things the festival consists of sit in the same frame: the bowl that is eaten from, and the lamps that light the whole street.
