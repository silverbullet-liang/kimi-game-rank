# Valentine's Day

## Overview

14 February, also called Saint Valentine's Day. It is observed in many countries as a day for couples and for people in love. It is not a public holiday anywhere and has no single organiser; its practices have grown up through commerce and custom rather than by decree.

## Origins

The day carries the name of a Christian saint, Valentine, but which Valentine and what he did is told in more than one version, and what can be documented is limited, so usually only the name connection is asserted. The link between 14 February and romantic love is generally traced to fourteenth-century England: Chaucer's poem *The Parliament of Fowls* is considered among the earliest English works to present the date as the day birds choose their mates, and the idea of a lovers' day then settled in Europe. From the mid-nineteenth century printed cards became common, and the day turned from custom into something that could be bought; the pairing of roses, chocolate, cards and a dinner was assembled in layers after that.

## Customs

1. **Roses**: red ones most often; the meanings attached to stem counts and colours are mostly later additions. Florists sell far more on this day than on any other.
2. **Chocolate and sweets**: Japan developed its own system, distinguishing chocolate for a partner, for colleagues and for oneself, each with its own name and meaning.
3. **Cards and messages**: signed or unsigned; the unsigned card was a common device in early English practice.
4. **Dinner and proposals**: restaurants offer set menus for two, and many couples choose the day to propose or to register a marriage.
5. **Public display**: hearts and red decorations appear in streets, malls and on campuses, and declarations of affection cluster on social platforms.

## Story

The best-known version of the Valentine story runs like this: a Roman emperor forbade soldiers to marry, a priest named Valentine continued to marry young couples in secret, and was executed for it. Before his death he wrote to a blind girl he had come to know in prison, signing the letter "from your Valentine". The story exists in several versions with different details, and neither the date nor the identities can be confirmed; it belongs to legend. What is certain is that the signature survived, and became the English way of closing an anonymous love letter.

## Theme

The skin draws a rose on a white ground in a single continuous line, the petals left unfilled. No red, no shading — only one line travelling around and back to complete the flower. Valentine's Day is usually drawn full; this reverses it and leaves the flower at the stage before colour, because what it describes is not the warm part but the moment before anything has been said.
