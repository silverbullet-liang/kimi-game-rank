# Zhongyuan Festival

## Overview

The fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month is the Zhongyuan Festival. In Daoist usage it is Zhongyuan, in Buddhist usage the Ullambana festival, and among ordinary families most often simply the half of the seventh month. Its content is the remembrance of ancestors and of the dead, and it was one of the old observances of the sacrificial calendar, along with New Year's Eve, Qingming and Chongyang. Japan, the Korean peninsula and Vietnam keep observances of related origin under different names and on different dates.

## Origins

The name Zhongyuan comes from the Daoist teaching of the three officials: the fifteenth of the first month is Shangyuan, the fifteenth of the seventh is Zhongyuan and the fifteenth of the tenth is Xiayuan, belonging to the officials of Heaven, Earth and Water. On Zhongyuan the official of Earth is said to pardon offences, and rites for the dead were held on that day. The Buddhist account comes from the Ullambana Sutra, which tells of Maudgalyayana making offerings so that his mother might be released from suffering. In the seasonal records of the Northern and Southern Dynasties the fifteenth of the seventh month already carried both Ullambana offerings and visits to graves; the Jingchu suishi ji of the Liang period notes that on this day monks, nuns and lay people alike set out basins of offerings to the Buddhas. After the Tang and Song the Daoist, Buddhist and popular strands merged into the form now seen.

## Customs

1. **Ancestral offerings**: a table is set at home with incense, food and paper money burned, and family members bow in order of seniority.
2. **River lanterns**: a paper or wooden base with a candle inside is set on a river or lake and left to drift.
3. **Ullambana offerings**: temples hold services with chanting and the distribution of food, and worshippers bring basins of offerings.
4. **Universal offerings**: in some places sheds are raised in the streets, with platforms for the unattached dead and tall lantern poles.
5. **Night rites**: Daoist and Buddhist altars are set for those who have no descendants to make offerings, with chanting that lasts for hours.

## Story

The best known passage is the one in the Ullambana Sutra. As the sutra tells it, Maudgalyayana saw with his powers that his mother had fallen into the realm of hungry ghosts and could take no food, and he asked the Buddha how she might be saved. The Buddha told him that the fifteenth of the seventh month, when the monks' retreat ends, was the day to place food of a hundred flavours in basins and offer it to monks of the ten directions, and that by their power his mother could be released. He did so, and she was released. The sutra circulated widely in China and is the usual source given for the Ullambana service. On the Daoist side there is the pardon granted by the official of Earth; the two accounts have long run side by side, families often do not separate them, and offerings and rites borrow from one another.

## Theme

This skin holds a single river lantern: one lamp on the water, with a soft ring of light beneath it and three ripples, all made of glow rather than outline. The lamp is the only bright place on the page; the light spreads outward from beneath it and thins with distance, and the ripples are three paler arcs, each looser than the one inside it. There is no bank, no moon and no one in the picture, and the foot of the page is left as dark water without an edge.
