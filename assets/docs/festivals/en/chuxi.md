# Chinese New Year's Eve

## Overview

The last day of the lunar year: the thirtieth of the twelfth month in a long month, the twenty-ninth in a short one, and in common speech always "the big thirtieth". It joins the Spring Festival that begins the next morning, and it holds the densest cluster of rituals in the year — the reunion dinner, the couplets, the night watch, the gifts of money to children.

## Origins

The name is usually traced to the *Fengtu Ji* of Zhou Chu in the Western Jin, which records that on New Year's Eve the household stayed awake until dawn, "which is called watching the year out" — so by the Jin dynasty the vigil was already established. *Chu* means to remove or to hand over: the old year is removed at this night and another takes its place in the morning. The *Jingchu Suishiji* of the Liang dynasty likewise describes households preparing food and gathering to see in the new year. Most of what happens on the night serves one idea, that of receiving: sending the old year off and letting the new one in, which is why the lamps stay lit, someone stays awake, and food is left over.

## Customs

1. **The reunion dinner**: the whole family at one table, with dishes to spare — fish often kept head and tail intact as a wish for surplus year after year; dumplings in the north, shaped like ingots and eaten at midnight.
2. **Couplets and the character for blessing**: paired lines on the door frame, door gods and the character *fu* on the panels, often hung upside down because "upside down" sounds like "arrived".
3. **Watching the year out**: lamps left burning, the family talking, watching programmes or playing cards until midnight, when elders give children red envelopes of money.
4. **Ancestral offerings**: food, incense and paper burned before the meal, inviting the ancestors home for the festival; in some places an empty bowl and chopsticks are set out.
5. **Firecrackers**: once used to drive off evil and greet the year; in recent years many cities have designated no-firework zones and restricted hours, and practice has changed with them.

## Story

The best-known tale explains the night through a beast called Nian. It is said that a monster of that name came out on the last night of the year to harm people, and that it feared red, firelight and loud noise. So people pasted red paper on their doors, lit lamps, burned bamboo, and stayed awake all night on guard. When dawn came and the beast had not appeared, they congratulated one another. The story has no reliable textual source and comes from oral tradition, but it accounts for the three elements of the night — red, fire, and staying awake — in a single narrative, which is why it spread so widely.

## Theme

The skin draws a clock face with its hands at the end of their travel, and beside it a paper square with the character for blessing. The marks on the dial are the whole of the year; the hand rests in the last division. The blessing is pasted next to it, ready for the next one. New Year's Eve is the instant both are true at once: the old time has run out, and the new wish is already on the door.
