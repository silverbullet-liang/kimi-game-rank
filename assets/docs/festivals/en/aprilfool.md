# April Fools' Day

## Overview

1 April, also called All Fools' Day. It is not a public holiday in any country, has no single organiser and no fixed ritual. There is only one working rule: on this day people play jokes and stage harmless hoaxes, and the person taken in is called an April fool.

## Origins

There is no settled origin, and several explanations stand side by side. The most commonly repeated one concerns sixteenth-century France: the new year was once counted from the end of March and the beginning of April, then moved to 1 January, and those who went on exchanging new-year gifts in early April were laughed at as April fools, with real presents replaced by joke ones. Another account traces the day to the Roman festival of Hilaria or to medieval feasts of misrule, arguing that the opening of April was always a time when the usual order was allowed to turn over. None of these has decisive evidence, and the question is still argued.

## Customs

1. **Jokes before noon**: the old rule in Britain and France was that the joking stopped at midday, and anyone still at it after lunch was himself the fool. Few keep to it now.
2. **Fake news in the media**: newspapers, radio, television and websites publish invented reports on this day, and readers get several lines in before noticing. The tradition is well over a century old.
3. **Fake gifts and messages**: an empty box wrapped as a present, a note written as an urgent notice, a sham email to a colleague — all low-cost forms.
4. **Brand "launches"**: for the past two decades many companies have announced a product that does not exist, as a display of invention.
5. **Limits**: the accepted line is that nothing should hurt, cost money or create real alarm; a joke past that line is treated as an incident, not a festival.

## Story

On 1 April 1957 the BBC current-affairs programme *Panorama* broadcast a three-minute report saying that the spaghetti harvest in Ticino, Switzerland, had been unusually good that year, with farmers taking bundles of spaghetti down from the trees; the film showed women lifting the strands from the branches into baskets. Spaghetti was still an unfamiliar food in Britain, and many viewers believed it, telephoning the BBC to ask how to grow spaghetti at home. The programme's advice was to put a strand in a tin of tomato sauce and "hope for the best". The report has been cited ever since as the best-known joke of the day.

## Theme

The skin shows a single question mark turned upside down, and one dashed line, in very plain colour. A question mark is the shape of a question; inverted, it becomes the shape of something unreasonable. The dashed line is a line that ought to be continuous and has been broken. April Fools' Day needs no red and no gold — only a symbol given a small twist.
