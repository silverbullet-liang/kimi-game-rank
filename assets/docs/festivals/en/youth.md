# Youth Day

## Overview

Youth Day falls on 4 May and is the festival of young people in China. In 1939 the Northwest Youth Association for National Salvation in the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region designated 4 May as Chinese Youth Day, and on 23 December 1949 the Government Administration Council of the Central People's Government formally announced the decision. Under current holiday rules, young people aged fourteen and over have a half day off.

## Origins

The day comes from the May Fourth Movement of 1919. The Paris Peace Conference opened in January that year; China's claim to recover its rights in Shandong was not supported, and at the end of April the conference decided to transfer Germany's interests in Shandong to Japan. When the news reached China, students demonstrated in Beijing on 4 May, and student strikes, shop closures and workers' strikes spread across the country. In 1939 the Northwest Youth Association set 4 May as Youth Day to carry on that tradition, and in December 1949 the Government Administration Council announced it formally.

## Customs

1. **League and coming-of-age ceremonies**: schools hold oath ceremonies for new League members and ceremonies for students turning eighteen.
2. **Awards and forums**: the May Fourth Youth Medal, selection of outstanding members, and discussions with youth representatives.
3. **Volunteering**: community service, teaching in rural schools, environmental work and science outreach.
4. **Culture and sport**: choirs, speeches, long-distance runs and ball games, mostly on the theme of youth.
5. **Visits to historic sites**: the Red Building of Peking University and memorial halls of the May Fourth Movement.

## Story

On the afternoon of 4 May 1919 more than three thousand students from a dozen Beijing schools gathered at Tiananmen and marched toward the Dongjiaominxiang legation quarter to protest the handling of the Shandong question at the Paris Peace Conference. During the march the house of Cao Rulin at Zhaojialou was set on fire, and dozens of students were arrested. As the news spread, students in Tianjin, Shanghai and Guangzhou struck in turn; from June, Shanghai workers went on strike and shops closed. The Beiyang government released the arrested students that month, and the Chinese delegation in the end did not sign the Paris peace treaty. The sequence is recorded at length in the newspapers and student memoirs of the time.
