# International Workers' Day

## Overview

Observed on 1 May and known in Chinese as May First International Labour Day. The Paris congress of the Second International resolved in 1889 that 1 May be kept as International Workers' Day, and in December 1949 the Government Administration Council of the Central People's Government made it a statutory holiday in China. More than eighty countries take a public holiday on the date.

## Origins

On 1 May 1886 workers in Chicago and other American cities struck for the eight-hour day, and clashes followed, remembered as the Haymarket affair. In July 1889 the Second International met in Paris and resolved that 1 May be observed each year as International Workers' Day, first marked in 1890. Chinese workers marked the day from 1918, with marches and meetings in Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou in 1920, and in December 1949 the Government Administration Council fixed 1 May as a statutory holiday.

## Customs

1. **Holidays and rallies**: most countries close for the day, and unions hold marches, meetings and concerts.
2. **Honouring workers**: since 1989 China has recognised national model workers and outstanding employees roughly every five years.
3. **Travel and family**: a break of about five days, used for trips or for going home.
4. **Workplace events**: choirs, evening programmes and skills competitions on the theme of labour.

## Story

On 1 May 1886 tens of thousands of workers in Chicago took to the streets to demand an eight-hour working day. On 3 May police fired on striking workers, and on the evening of 4 May, at a meeting in Haymarket Square, a bomb was thrown and police opened fire; several people died. Afterwards a number of labour organisers were tried and executed. The 1889 resolution of the Second International was taken with these events in view. Accounts of the sequence differ in detail from one labour history to another, but the link between 1 May and the eight-hour day was set from then on.

## Theme

On a deep ochre ground a wrench and a hammer cross each other, drawn with straight lines, without ornament or extra shadow. These are the two commonest things on an industrial site, yet their overlap looks like a handshake: one tightens, one drives home, and both describe the making of a thing.
