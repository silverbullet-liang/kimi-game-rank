# Halloween

## Overview

The night of 31 October, the eve of All Saints' Day on 1 November. The word is a contraction of All Hallows' Eve, in which "hallow" means a saint. The night is kept mainly in the United States, the British Isles, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. This skin is forced to a dark palette for the whole of its window.

## Origins

The beginning is usually traced to the Celtic festival of Samhain. The Celts counted 1 November as the start of the year, and the night before was held to be a time when the boundary between the living and the dead grew thin; people lit bonfires and put on animal skins and masks to keep wandering spirits at a distance. In the eighth century the church fixed All Saints' Day on 1 November, which made the night before All Hallows' Eve, and the name followed. Medieval Britain and Ireland also had souling, going from door to door for a soul cake in return for prayers for the dead, and guising, going masked from house to house to perform. In the nineteenth century, and especially after the Irish famine of the 1840s, Irish immigrants carried the night to North America, where it became a community holiday; the phrase trick or treat settled into American use in the 1930s.

## Customs

1. **Costumes**: dressing as ghosts or as characters, with a bowl of sweets kept by the door.
2. **Trick or treat**: children knock and say the words, the household gives sweets, and both sides keep to the same bargain.
3. **Jack-o'-lanterns**: pumpkins hollowed out and cut into faces, lit from inside and set on the step.
4. **Apple bobbing**: apples floating in a basin, taken without hands, a survival of an older game of divination.
5. **Haunted houses and ghost stories**: temporary haunted rooms, fancy-dress parties, horror films run late.

## Story

The lantern comes from an Irish tale about a man nicknamed Stingy Jack. It is said that he tricked the devil twice, and that when he died he was admitted neither to heaven nor to hell, and so wanders the world carrying a hollowed-out turnip with a coal inside it; people called it Jack of the Lantern. The Irish first carved turnips or beetroots, and immigrants to North America turned to pumpkins, which were more plentiful and easier to cut. The tale comes down in Irish oral tradition and its date of formation can no longer be fixed, but it ties the lamp to a man whom neither side would take.

## Theme

The skin works on a dark ground with orange and purple set against each other, the orange gathered in one place and the purple scattered to the corners. Silhouette ornaments — bats, bare branches, an arch — give only the shape and no detail. For the length of its window the palette stays dark, so that a page opened at noon is still held down to the same low light.
