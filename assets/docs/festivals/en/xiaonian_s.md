# Little New Year in Southern China

## Overview

The twenty-fourth day of the twelfth lunar month, kept as Little New Year across most of southern China and also called the Kitchen God Festival or the dust-sweeping day. In parts of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai region the night before New Year's Eve is called "little New Year's Eve" as well, and the two names coexist. Whatever the name, the day starts the same work: sweeping the house, buying festival goods and getting the home ready.

## Origins

The one-day gap between north and south is usually explained by an old saying: officials on the twenty-third, commoners on the twenty-fourth, boat people on the twenty-fifth. After the Qing dynasty the north mostly followed the official date of the twenty-third, while the south kept the twenty-fourth, and so the two halves of the country still differ. The offering to the kitchen god is itself a very old rite: the *Book of Rites* lists a stove among the seven sacrifices, and the *Analects* warns that it is better to flatter the stove than the household shrine. The day was also when old-style firms closed their ledgers, so debts were called in and settled before it.

## Customs

1. **Sweeping the dust**: bamboo brooms and feather dusters work from the beams down to the corners; bedding is unpicked and washed, windows and doors scrubbed, a year of dust removed in one go.
2. **The kitchen god's send-off**: sugar, cakes and wine before the stove; in parts of the south the offerings also include bean curd, sugarcane and water chestnuts.
3. **Washing and drying**: on a fine winter day bedding and clothes fill the yard, and people get their hair cut and bathe — clean, to meet the New Year.
4. **Festival food**: cured meats, sausages and dried fish; rice cakes, pounded glutinous rice and steamed rice, since rice products weigh more heavily in southern New Year supplies.
5. **Couplets and blessings**: couplets written by hand or by a neighbour, red paper cut into door gods and the character for blessing, pasted on doors and windows.

## Story

The folk explanation of dust-sweeping turns on a pun. The word for dust sounds the same as the word for "old" or "stale", so sweeping dust is sweeping out the stale: the past year's leftover troubles go out the door with it. That is why the sweeping starts at the roof beam and works toward the entrance, always outward. The broom matters too. Bamboo grows everywhere in the south, so branches are cut and bound into a long, stiff-handled broom that reaches the beams and rafters and does not fall apart with use. No classical text records this, but every year the brooms by the doors and the bedding in the yards are the story still being lived.

## Theme

The skin leans a bamboo broom against the left side of the frame, with four small motes of dust drifting up beside it. The broom is worn and the marks of its sweeping remain; the dust is fine and stays close together. Sweeping is a quiet action with no sound to it, which suits the day: no celebration, only a year cleared away to make room for what comes after.
