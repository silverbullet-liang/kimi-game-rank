# International Museum Day

## Overview

Observed on 18 May and established by the International Council of Museums (ICOM) in 1977. Museums around the world mark the date with exhibitions, free admission, lectures and public programmes built around an annual theme, so that more people come to know the work they do.

## Origins

ICOM was founded in Paris in 1946. At its 11th General Assembly in Moscow in 1977 the council resolved to declare 18 May 1977 the first International Museum Day and to set a theme for the date each year. The Chinese Museums Association joined ICOM in 1983 and has held related events annually since then, with recent themes turning on the social role of museums.

## Customs

1. **Free admission**: many museums waive entry fees on the day or extend their opening hours.
2. **Themed exhibitions**: special shows are planned around the theme announced by ICOM.
3. **Late openings**: museum nights with lighting, guided tours and performances running together.
4. **Talks and workshops**: conservation demonstrations, ink rubbing, pottery and family tours.
5. **Work with schools**: class visits, and museum staff teaching in classrooms.

## Story

In November 1946 ICOM was founded in Paris, and its statutes defined a museum as a non-profit, permanent institution in the service of society and its development, which collects, conserves, researches, communicates and exhibits the evidence of humanity and its environment. The definition has been revised since, and remains the common ground of museum work. At the 11th General Assembly in Moscow in 1977 the council decided that an international day should be set aside to bring museums and their publics a little closer, and 18 May was chosen for it.

## Theme

A glass display case with a slanted reflection running across its front, and inside it a plain clay jar with no pattern at all. The reflection cuts the case in two, one half transparent and the other nothing but light; the jar sits beneath that band, its outline clear while the detail is screened off by the glass. Looking at exhibits tends to work this way: first your own reflection, then the object.
