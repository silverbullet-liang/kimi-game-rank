# Winter Clothing Festival

## Overview

The first day of the tenth lunar month, also called the Tenth Month Festival, the Ancestors' Festival or the Robe-Giving Festival. In popular usage the day stands alongside Qingming and the Ghost Festival as one of the days in the year for tending to the dead. In the north, families visit graves and burn paper clothing for the deceased, an act called "sending winter clothes". The tenth month is also the beginning of winter, after which the weather turns cold.

## Origins

The giving out of winter clothing is an old matter. The *Book of Songs*, in the Odes of Bin, has the line "in the seventh month the Fire Star sinks, in the ninth month we give out clothes", meaning that by the ninth month it is time to prepare for the cold. In the second year of the Tianbao era, 743, the Tang court issued an ordinance that clothes be offered at the imperial tombs on the first day of the ninth month each year, as recorded in the *Tang Da Zhao Ling Ji*, and the ordinance influenced the popular practice of bringing garments to graves. Because winter does not begin until the tenth month and the ninth seemed early, the Song moved the custom to the first of the tenth. Meng Yuanlao records that in Kaifeng, from late in the ninth month, paper garments, boots, hats and cloth were on sale "for burning on the first of the tenth", and that on that day "officials and commoners alike went out of the city to make offerings at graves". Zhou Mi, writing of Lin'an, notes that "on the first of the tenth the city goes out to the graves with cotton robes and paper clothes". By the Yuan the name Winter Clothing Festival appears in the *Xijin Zhi*. The Ming *Dijing Jingwu Lue* is fullest: paper shops cut five-coloured paper into men's and women's garments about a foot long, wrote the name and generation of the dead on them as one addresses a letter, and each household called out and burned them at the door at night.

## Customs

1. **Sending winter clothes**: garments cut from five-coloured paper, packed in paper bags with the names of the dead written on them, burned at the grave or at the door.
2. **Visiting graves**: tidying the mound, setting out food, burning paper, in the manner of Qingming but simpler.
3. **Giving clothes**: winter garments and quilts for the elders of the household, a layer of the custom still practised.
4. **Lighting the stove**: the day traditionally marked the first heating of the house in the north, another mark that winter has begun.
5. **Offering the new harvest**: in parts of the north, millet or dumplings made from the new crop are set before the ancestors.

## Story

The best-known story attached to the day is that of Meng Jiangnu. It is said that her husband was taken away to build the Great Wall and no word came; she sewed winter clothes and walked a thousand li to find him, only to learn that he was dead and that his bones had been built into the wall, whereupon her weeping brought the wall down. The earliest record is a brief note on the wife of Qi Liang, and the tale grew through opera and ballad-singing, taking different shapes in different periods; its origin can no longer be fixed. Popular custom joins it to the first of the tenth month, and both say the same thing: the cold has come and the clothes must arrive.
