# Mid-Autumn Festival

## Overview

The Mid-Autumn Festival falls on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month. The eighth month is the middle month of autumn and the fifteenth day is the middle of that month, which is how the name arose; it is also called the Moon Festival, the Eighth Month Festival and the Reunion Festival. On 20 May 2006 it was placed on the first national list of intangible cultural heritage with State Council approval, and since 2008 it has been a statutory public holiday.

## Origins

The phrase "mid-autumn" appears as early as the *Rites of Zhou*, in the chapter on the office of music: "at mid-spring, by day, they beat the earthen drum and pipe the songs of Bin to meet the heat; at mid-autumn, by night, they meet the cold in the same way." There the phrase means the eighth month and a seasonal rite, not a festival. Tang poetry already has many poems on the moon of the fifteenth night, among them Du Fu's "Moon of the Fifteenth Night" and Wang Jian's "Watching the Moon on the Fifteenth Night". By the Song the day had become a festival shared by every level of society. Meng Yuanlao, in fascicle eight of the *Dongjing Meng Hua Lu*, describes the Northern Song capital: "on the night of mid-autumn, wealthy households deck out their terraces, and common folk race to take the upper floors of taverns to watch the moon." Wu Zimu's *Meng Liang Lu* is plainer still: "the fifteenth of the eighth month is the Mid-Autumn Festival; the three months of autumn are exactly half gone, and so it is called mid-autumn." The mooncake settled into its role later; Tian Rucheng, writing in the Ming, records that "on the fifteenth of the eighth month people send mooncakes to one another, taking the meaning of reunion." From the Ming and Qing onward the day stood beside the New Year, Qingming and the Double Fifth as one of the four great festivals.

## Customs

1. **Watching and worshipping the moon**: a table set towards the rising moon with melon, pomegranates and mooncakes; in older households the women of the family made the offerings.
2. **Mooncakes**: always round, cut into shares for everyone at the table; the shape and the dividing both point at the same thing.
3. **Osmanthus**: the flower opens exactly now, and in the south it goes into wine, sugar and cakes.
4. **Lanterns**: children carry rabbit lanterns through the streets; on the waterways of the south small lamps are set afloat.
5. **The tidal bore**: on the Qiantang at Haining the mid-autumn bore is watched from the bank, a custom recorded since the Tang and Song.

## Story

The best-known tale is that of Chang'e. It is said that Hou Yi obtained an elixir of deathlessness from the Queen Mother of the West, and that Chang'e, his wife, swallowed it and flew up to the moon, where she has stayed. The *Huainanzi*, a Western Han work, already records that "Heng'e stole the medicine and fled to the moon", so the story is at least two thousand years old, though each age told it differently. Later tellings add a jade rabbit pounding medicine and Wu Gang felling a cassia tree that closes up again as fast as he cuts it, so that the tree never comes down. None of this is history, but it is the earliest layer of how the moon was imagined.

## Theme

The skin puts one full moon high on the page, ringed by a pale halo, and draws a single thin cloud across its face. The cloud does not cover the moon, it only crosses it, and the picture stops there: the round and the passing, the gathered and the thing that will not stay, on the same night.
