# Laba Festival

## Overview

The eighth day of the twelfth lunar month, also called Laba. *La* was originally the name of the year-end sacrifice; the day is both an occasion for offering to ancestors and gods at the close of the year and, in popular reckoning, the point where the New Year season begins. Once Laba is past, shopping for the festival, cleaning the house and writing couplets follow in sequence.

## Origins

The year-end *la* sacrifice existed before the Qin dynasty, offered to ancestors and to a range of gods in thanks for the harvest. The *Jingchu Suishiji* by Zong Lin of the Liang dynasty notes that "the eighth day of the twelfth month is the la day", which shows the date was fixed by the Southern and Northern Dynasties. Later the Buddhist commemoration of the Buddha's enlightenment merged with it, and monasteries cooked congee on this day as an offering and gave it to lay people. According to the *Mengliang Lu* of the Southern Song, monasteries on the eighth day "prepared five-flavour congee, called laba congee", so by the Song the dish was already widespread. In the Ming and Qing it moved from monastery kitchens into ordinary homes, with ingredients multiplying according to what each region grew.

## Customs

1. **Cooking laba congee**: rice, beans, dates, chestnuts, lotus seeds, peanuts and longan, eight kinds or many more; offered to ancestors first, shared with neighbours, then eaten by the family, with some deliberately left over.
2. **Pickling laba garlic**: in the north, peeled garlic steeped in rice vinegar and sealed until New Year's Eve, when the cloves have turned jade green and the vinegar has taken the garlic's bite; eaten with dumplings.
3. **Laba noodles**: in parts of the Guanzhong plain of Shaanxi, noodles with a bean and vegetable sauce are eaten at dawn instead of congee.
4. **Giving congee away**: temples, shrines and some shops distribute congee; at home a bowl and chopsticks for the ancestors still stand on the altar table.
5. **Starting the New Year work**: from this day people buy festival goods, cure meat and write couplets; the saying goes that once Laba is over, the New Year has begun.

## Story

The best-known account of laba congee links it to the Buddha's enlightenment. It is said that after years of austerity the Buddha-to-be had grown thin without attaining insight; a herdsgirl offered him milk rice, his strength returned, and he sat under the bodhi tree until he understood. In Chinese tradition that day is recorded as the eighth of the twelfth month, so monasteries cook congee in offering. A plainer story runs alongside it: poor households at the end of the year scraped together whatever grain was left at the bottom of the jars and boiled it into a mixed porridge, which turned out to taste good. The first account entered monastic ritual; the second stayed on the kitchen stove.

## Theme

The skin draws a coarse clay bowl with seven grains suspended above its rim and a faint thread of steam rising. The roughness of the clay, the loose scatter of grain and the lightness of the steam are what this day actually consists of: the year nearly spent, the last few handfuls gathered and boiled into one hot bowl.
